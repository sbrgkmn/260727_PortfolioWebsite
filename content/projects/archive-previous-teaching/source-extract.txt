Pedagogical Premise 
Morphing Skins explored architectural design through 
a pedagogical reversal. Instead of beginning with site, 
program, massing, and then envelope, the studio 
began with surface. Students first developed analog 
techniques through paper folding, cutting, curling, 
faceting, crumpling, triangulating, and tessellating. 
These operations were treated not as decorative 
effects, but as tectonic systems capable of producing 
structure, enclosure, spatial depth, light modulation, 
and programmatic organization. 
 
The studio asked students to think of the building en-
velope as an active architectural agent. Surface was not 
understood as a thin layer applied after design deci-
sions were made, but as a generative medium through 
which design decisions could emerge. Through this 
outside-in process, students learned how a surface 
technique could become a façade system, how a façade 
system could inform massing, and how massing could 
then be integrated with circulation, structure, and 
program. 
 
The pedagogical goal was to help students connect 
material experimentation with architectural resolu-
tion. Paper became a low-cost research medium for 
testing geometric behavior, pattern, repetition, thick-
ness, fold direction, aggregation, and transformation. 
Digital modeling then allowed students to translate 
these discoveries into scalable systems that could be 
applied to a real architectural site. 
 
Methodology 
The semester was organized in two major phases. In 
the first half, students worked in groups to develop pa-
per-based tectonic systems. Each group investigated a 
specific formal operation and produced physical mod-
els, diagrams, digital translations, rule sets, and matri-
ces of variation. The emphasis was on rigor: students 
were asked to identify repeatable rules, test variations, 
document transformations, through diagrams and 
digital models and understand how a surface opera-
tion could become a design method. 
 
In the second half, students applied these research 
systems to an architectural project for the Carolina 
Theatre site. The shift from abstract surface research 
to architectural design required students to adapt 
their techniques to the demands of an urban context, 
public program, building enclosure, structure, circu-
lation, and interior experience. Projects moved from 
surface to façade, from façade to massing, and from 
massing to programmatic integration. The final goal 
was to produce large physical models that translated 
the performative aspects of folding towards various 
building systems. 
  
Phase I: Paper Tectonics 
Students began with analog experiments using paper 
as a construction medium. Through distinct research 
tracks: folding, crumpling, curling, faceting, trian-
gulating, and tessellating, they produced families of 
surface studies and developed diagrams explaining the 
rules behind each transformation. 
 
Phase II: Surface Tectonics 
Students translated paper techniques into digital mod-
els and larger physical systems. This phase focused on 
connecting individual surface units into networks, 
testing depth and scale, and producing model matri-
ces that demonstrated formal variation. For midterm, 
student groups were asked to develop a massing model 
for a given volume that will become integrated into 
the site / program in the second half of the studio.  
 
Phase III: Site and Massing Integration 
After midterm, students transformed their surface sys-
tems to the given Carolina Theatre site and program. 
They used their research to develop massing strategies, 
façade systems, and spatial organizations that respond-
ed to the urban block and public program. Overall 
approach for design was to have the abstract massing 
and structure drive the program integration into the 
volume.  
 
Phase IV: Program and Façade Design 
The final phase integrated envelope, structure, circu-
lation, and program. Students produced architectural 
proposals through large physical models, plans, sec-
tions, elevations, renderings, and diagrams explaining 
how their surface systems shaped the building as a 
whole. 
 
Student Project Topics 
The student work presented in the following pages 
explored a range of surface-based design techniques, 
including folded pleats, structural ribs, spatial parti-
tions, tessellated and triangulated façade systems, lay-
ered envelopes, faceted massing strategies, and curled 
or crumpled skins. The work presented here includes 
examples from the studio-wide scope, along with 
selected projects from the coordinator’s section.  
 
Learning Outcomes 
In this studio, students learned to develop design 
systems through material experimentation, document 
formal operations through diagrams and rule sets, 
translate analog techniques into digital models, and 
apply abstract surface research to an architectural 
project. The studio strengthened their ability to move 
between hand-making and computational modeling, 
between surface and structure, and between formal ex-
perimentation and architectural resolution. They were 
provided with weekly tutorials on translating paper 
folding techniques to parametric and digital modeling 
approaches. The final projects demonstrate how an 
envelope-first design process can generate buildings 
in which façade, massing, structure, circulation, and 
program emerge as interconnected systems rather than 
sequential stages. More broadly, the pedagogy chal-
lenged conventional third-year studio expectations by 
shifting the emphasis from designing a discrete “build-
ing” toward developing analog-computational systems 
capable of driving architectural form, tectonic expres-
sion, and building-systems integration.
