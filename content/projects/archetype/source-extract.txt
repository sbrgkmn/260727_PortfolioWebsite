ARCHETYPE SYMPOSIUM AND BOOKLET
Symposium 
In Fall 2025, I organized and moderated Archetype: 
Building Intelligence through Generative Archi-
tecture, the first Marley P. Carroll Symposium on 
AI and Architecture at the UNC Charlotte School of 
Architecture. The symposium was conceived as a pub-
lic forum for examining how artificial intelligence is 
reshaping architectural design, pedagogy, authorship, 
and professional practice. Rather than treating AI as a 
technical novelty, the event framed it through the lon-
ger disciplinary concept of the archetype: a recurring 
spatial, formal, and organizational pattern that persists 
through historical transformation. 
 
The one-day symposium brought together national 
keynote speakers, faculty, students, and professional 
practitioners to discuss the transition from draw-
ing-centered design workflows to data-rich, mod-
el-based, and AI-assisted environments. Keynote 
lectures by Andrew Kudless and Rob Otani addressed 
emerging intersections between generative systems, 
architectural intelligence, material practice, and the 
AEC industry. These lectures were followed by faculty 
responses and an AI Forum with local and national 
practitioners, including participants from academia, 
architectural practice, and technology-oriented design 
research. The event also included student work and 
exhibition material, positioning the symposium as 
both a scholarly conversation and a pedagogical plat-
form. 
 
My role involved developing the symposium theme, 
coordinating the program structure, inviting and 
communicating with speakers and panelists, moder-
ating discussions, planning the student work exhibit 
and connecting the event to broader curricular and 
research conversations within the School of Architec-
ture. The symposium supported the School’s growing 
engagement with computation and AI by creating a 
shared space for faculty, students, and practitioners 
to discuss the cultural, ethical, and methodological 
implications of generative technologies.

Booklet 
Following the event, I led the development of the 
Archetype booklet, an A5 publication of approxi-
mately 128 pages that extends the symposium into a 
durable institutional record. The booklet is organized 
into three parts: a theoretical and historical fram-
ing of archetype and generative intelligence; faculty 
contributions that situate archetypal thinking within 
contemporary architectural practice; and student 
work that demonstrates how these ideas are carried 
forward through teaching, experimentation, and 
design research. The publication includes symposium 
documentation, keynote summaries, edited discus-
sions, faculty essays, student projects, visual material, 
contributor bios, and reflective framing texts. 
 
T ogether, the symposium and booklet transformed 
a one-day event into a broader service contribution: 
a public forum, a pedagogical resource, a record of 
faculty and student work, and a platform for position-
ing UNC Charlotte’s School of Architecture within 
contemporary conversations on AI and architectural 
education. This work reflects my broader service 
commitment to building connections among compu-
tation, curriculum, public discourse, and professional 
practice.

Architecture has long been sustained by recurring spatial 
structures that transmit knowledge across generations. 
Architectural forms such as courtyards, basilicas, hypo-
style halls, and vernacular houses reappear across cultures 
shaped by different climates and social conditions, yet they 
reveal recognizable patterns of organization. These forms 
are typically described through the language of typology; 
however, the concept of the archetype offers a deeper 
framework through which the persistence and transforma-
tion of architectural intelligence may be understood.
An archetype is a latent organizational pattern or principle 
embedded within form. Rather than a fixed model to be 
replicated, it operates as a generative structure capable 
of producing variation. Much like homologous vertebrate 
limbs, in which the hand, paw, paddle, and wing retain 
comparable skeletal patterns while adapting to different 
functions,1 architectural archetypes evolve through contin-
uous adaptation to material conditions, cultural contexts, 
and technological regimes. The built artifact therefore 
represents only one manifestation of a deeper entity that 
precedes and exceeds any singular manifestation.
Architecture is increasingly shaped by computational and 
data driven technologies through which the mechanisms 
of form generation, classification, and transformation are 
undergoing profound change. Generative algorithms, 
parametric modeling systems, and machine learning frame-
works now participate directly in the production of architec-
tural variation. Far from displacing historical architectural 
knowledge, these technologies reorganize the ways in 
which such knowledge is encoded, retrieved, and extended.

THE FREEDOM OF INCOHERENCE
Early stages of design are often characterized by ambigu-
ity. Sketches are imprecise, contradictory, and incomplete, 
yet they contain the seeds of future ideas. Traditional digital 
tools frequently struggle to support this stage because 
they require designers to commit to precise geometries 
and structured workflows too early.
Generative AI reintroduces a form of productive ambiguity. 
The images produced by diffusion models are often imper-
fect, surreal, or inconsistent. For designers, this apparent 
incoherence can become a source 
of inspiration.
Instead of resolving every detail 
immediately, architects can explore 
a wide range of conceptual direc-
tions. Images function as specu-
lative sketches rather than final 
representations.
Kudless suggests that the value of AI lies precisely in this 
capacity to sustain uncertainty. By allowing ideas to remain 
unresolved, generative tools help designers navigate the 
complex and contradictory conditions that shape architec-
tural thinking.
“Design does not begin with certainty— 
it begins with contradictions.”

RETHINKING DESIGN AND COMPUTATION
Sabri Gokmen: T o begin, I want to situate AI 
within the longer history of computation in archi-
tecture. For several decades, computation has 
served as a medium for representation, mod-
eling, scripting, and fabrication. But AI seems 
to shift computation toward something more 
generative, ambiguous, and collaborative. How 
has the role of computation changed, and does 
AI extend computational design or challenge it? 
 
Andrew Kudless: I have been a big user of compu-
tation since I was a student in the 1990s, and I found 
it incredibly empowering. Most of my time on the 
computer is spent in Grasshopper, writing code, or 
working through computational tools. I enjoy that, 
but there is also a frustrating side: spending hours 
solving esoteric problems like data trees, equa-
tions, software updates, or compatibility issues. 
 
Generative AI feels like the opposite end of that 
spectrum. “On the surface, it just works.” Y ou still 
need to understand some of the details, but there 
is a freedom in not having to manage a complex 
data tree or build every operation through code. 
Y ou can describe what you want. Most of the time 
you will not get exactly what you want, but you will 
get something that challenges your original think -
ing. For me, AI is more like a sketch, “something 
to help me work through ideas very, very quickly” 
before I dive back into complex code, Grass -
hopper models, or more structured systems. 
Rob Otani: I think it depends where you are in 
the landscape of design. If you are a designer, AI 
might feel freeing in the way Andrew describes. 
But if you are a computational designer, it can be 
disruptive. Computational designers are used 
to curating their design space very clearly, of -
ten through Grasshopper or other parametric 
tools. AI appears to create a design space au-
tomatically, so there is a sense of threat there. 
 
But I also think the best-case scenario is that AI 
expands what computational designers can do. 
In our office, computational designers are already 
using AI-assisted coding to create custom appli-
cations. So instead of replacing computational 
design, AI may push it from a relatively narrow 
area of parametric work toward a much broader 
field of validation, iteration, and design impact. 
 
Kyle Spence: From the standpoint of compu-
tation, there is an important historical parallel. 
In the 1990s, when computers became more 
common in design, there was a period when 
new geometries emerged that seemed un-
familiar, often described as blobs. They were 
exciting, but also difficult to rationalize, diffi-
cult to sell to clients, and difficult to construct.

PARAMETRIC DESIGN AND 
COMPUTATIONAL GEOMETRY
AUTOMATION AND RISE OF DATA
By the mid 2000s, computational design began to trans-
form the relationship between geometry and engineering. 
Parametric modeling environments allowed designers to 
generate complex structural configurations while maintain-
ing precise control over their underlying parameters.
For engineers, this shift required new 
forms of interdisciplinary collabo-
ration. Architects were increasingly 
using computational tools to explore 
complex geometries, and structural 
engineers had to develop new analyt-
ical techniques capable of evaluating 
these forms.
Otani describes this moment as a turn-
ing point in his own career. Exposure 
to computational design communities 
and emerging tools such as Grass-
hopper introduced new possibilities 
for integrating geometry, analysis, 
and fabrication within a unified digital 
workflow.
These developments expanded the 
role of engineers from purely analytical 
consultants into active collaborators in 
the generative process of design.
As computational tools matured, engineering practice in-
creasingly shifted toward data driven methods. Building In-
formation Modeling (BIM) environments allowed structural 
models to function not only as geometric representations 
but also as databases containing detailed information about 
materials, structural systems, and construction processes.
Within this environment, the structure 
of a building can be understood as a 
network of interdependent relation-
ships. Changes to one component 
propagate through the system, requir -
ing adjustments across multiple ele-
ments of the design.
Otani describes this condition us -
ing the analogy of a directed graph in 
which each structural decision influ-
ences a chain of subsequent design 
decisions. Engineering design there-
fore becomes a process of managing 
complex networks of dependencies.
Automation tools began to emerge 
as a way of navigating these relation-
ships. Scripts and custom applications 
allowed engineers to automate repetitive calculations and 
design processes, reducing the time required to evaluate 
structural alternatives.
"Computation changed the way 
engineers and architects could 
work together. Geometry, analysis, 
and design started to happen inside 
the same environment."

FRAMING AI
Sabri Gokmen: For this afternoon session, we 
are shifting from the theoretical framework of the 
morning lectures to a more practice-oriented dis -
cussion. This forum brings together practitioners, 
researchers, and educators who are actively en-
gaging artificial intelligence in the AEC industry, 
from design computation and digital fabrication 
to business strategy, construction technology, 
and regenerative systems. I want to begin broad-
ly: how has computation changed the way you 
think about design and problem-solving in your 
work, and how do you see AI taking that further? 
 
Kyong-Hee Kim: In my research, computation has 
changed the way we frame questions. We begin 
with a hypothesis: for instance, if we use AI-driv -
en sensor integration, can we improve a certain 
environmental performance by ten or twenty 
percent compared to experiments without AI? 
 
In our lab, we are working with microalgae as liv -
ing organisms that can sequester carbon and 
improve energy performance. Before using AI, 
we relied on basic sensor integration, data col-
lection, and trend analysis. We might study how 
light levels affect algae cell density, for example. 
With AI, we can monitor and curate data in real 
time across multiple factors: light levels, CO 2 lev-
els from occupants entering and leaving a space, 
nutrient dosing, pH, temperature, and airflow. The 
goal is to train algorithms that can predict the next 
adjustment, perhaps the next hour’s light level or 
CO2 flow. In that sense, AI is not only a design tool. 
It becomes a way to validate performance and 
test whether the hypothesis is actually working. 
 
Garrett Herbst: I’ll take that from the practice side. 
In our emerging technologies group, the mission 
is to identify the parts of our discipline that are 
mundane, repetitive, or administratively heavy, 
and then ask how technology can reduce that 
burden. The goal is not to automate the import -
ant parts of architecture away. It is to create more 
time for the work people are actually good at. 
 
For example, firm leaders are often promoted be-
cause they are excellent designers, practitioners, 
or business developers. But once they move into 
leadership, they are overwhelmed by staffing, fi-
nancials, emails, and administrative tasks. If AI can 
help reduce that load, then they can focus more 
on clients, design quality, and, maybe most impor -
tantly, mentoring the next generation. For us, the 
opportunity is to use AI to improve the quality of 
practice, not just to make things faster.

JEFFERSON ELLINGERA QUESTION OF AESTHETICS
The introduction of artificial intelligence (AI) into the disci-
pline of architecture is producing anxiety about the future 
of human contributions. However, this anxiety is largely 
being framed in the wrong terms. The prevailing question of 
whether AI will replace humans assumes that what humans 
contribute and what machines contribute are commensu-
rate – that the difference is one of degree rather than that 
of kind and this is a mistaken assumption. This mistake can 
in part be attributed to the discipline evolving over the last 
century to privilege humans that act like machines – this 
can be illustrated with so called evidence-based design for 
example.1 What AI systems produce (the machine) is prop-
ositional output: determinate solutions optimized against 
specifiable criteria, generated by the recombination of pat-
terns extracted from existing data. What the human con-
tributes (or will need to re-learn to contribute), and what will 
need to be foregrounded to maintain disciplinary relevance, 
is the capacity to evaluate whether something achieves a 
coherence, resonance or significance that exceeds what 
can be determined by its functional adequacy, logical 
correctness or empirical verification alone. This is aesthetic 
judgement. Graham Harman’s Object-Oriented Ontology 
(OOO) provides a philosophically precise account of aes-
thetic judgement and does so through a concept architec-
ture has become allergic to: beauty.

JONA THAN DESSI-OLIVEGENERA TIVE DESIGN THROUGH CUL TIVA TION
The work presented here emerges from ongoing research 
and fabrication at MycoMatters Lab, where architectural 
design is approached through the integration of material 
experimentation, computational methods, and spatial 
design practices. The design research and education work 
in the Lab focuses on the production of building-scale 
installations, prototypes, and acoustical systems. Central 
to this work is the cultivation of mycelium bio-composite 
materials, in which chitinous fungal hyphae act as a self-
generating adhesive that binds cellulosic substrates 
sourced from waste streams. These material processes 
are developed in parallel with computational design 
methods that generate formal variation through rule-based 
systems and support the evaluation of structural and 
environmental performance. 
Within this framework, acoustic diffuser panels provide a 
relevant point of entry into the design research conducted 
in the MycoMatters Lab. Diffuser panels are used to spread 
sound both spatially and temporally while maintaining 
total reflected energy. Although a wide range of non-flat 
geometries such as hemispheres, pyramids, arcs, and 
fractals can produce diffusion, acousticians commonly 
rely on number theory generated forms such as Quadratic 
Residue Diffuser (QRD) panels because they predictably 
scatter sound and prevent harsh reflections or echoes 
in a space. Composed of parallel wells of varying depths, 
designing phase grating QRD panels is parametric, as the 
depths of their wells correspond to optimal diffusive design 
frequencies.1 Within practice and the literature, QRDs are 
an ‘archetypal’ acoustic treatment commonly deployed as a 
straight extrusion of its frequency dependent cross sec-
tion, arranged in repeating patterns across entire surfaces.

ELIZABETH L. MCCORMICKTHE ARCHITECTURE OF LANGUAGE
The promise of generative architecture is seductive: more 
data, more knowledge, more optimization. Better forms, 
better systems, better futures. But this promise rests on a 
quiet assumption that intelligence can be extracted from 
existing conditions, formalized into models, and recom-
bined into something new. But what if that assumption is 
the problem?
Ask an image generator to produce a library and it will al-
most invariably return shelves of books. Shelving systems, 
stacks and spaces organized around the codex as the pri-
mary vessel of knowledge. The image will be coherent and 
legible, even persuasive. But it will also be constrained by a 
very specific epistemological framework: that knowledge 
is discrete, bounded, and stored in objects. That a library is 
a place where those objects are collected and accessed. 
The term “library” in itself activates a historically sediment-
ed set of associations that the model cannot easily es-
cape. In this sense, generative AI does not simply produce 
images, it reproduces categories. Generative systems then 
operate within the bounds of existing language, and there-
fore within the confines of existing paradigms.

instructive here because it frames categories not as neutral 
descriptions of reality, but as systems for organizing knowl-
edge. Though written decades before the emergence of 
generative AI, the categorical logic he critiques is precisely
