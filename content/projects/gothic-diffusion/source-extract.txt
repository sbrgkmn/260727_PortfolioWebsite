Gothic Diffusion
Gothic Diffusion is an AI-assisted computational design project ex-
hibited at Lambla Gallery that investigates the relationship between 
generative images, architectural form, and computational geometry. 
The project asks what it means for a machine to learn Gothic archi-
tecture as a system of ribs, tracery, rose windows, vault webs, point-
ed arches, clustered supports, and ornamental fragments. 
 
The project began with a curated image corpus of Gothic sections, 
elevations, tracery studies, rose windows, ornamental fragments, 
and plans. This dataset was used to fine-tune an SDXL model 
through LoRA training, then guided through ControlNet using 
line, depth, and edge-based conditions. The resulting image studies 
were extended into multi-view sequences and converted into ap-
proximate 3D meshes through image-to-mesh workflows. Selected 
outputs were fabricated as resin-printed artifacts. 
 
The project demonstrates the visual power of diffusion-based AI 
while exposing its architectural limits. The generated forms are 
atmospheric and spatially suggestive, yet they lack explicit geomet-
ric rules, editable feature histories, structural logic, and parametric 
relationships. Gothic Diffusion frames image generation as a critical 
research tool for testing the boundary between visual speculation 
and computationally legible architectural form.

The Vitruvian Turn: Why Generative Images Fail as Architectural Computation | 9 
5. Why Generative Images Fail as Architectural Computation 
Architecture is primarily a generative discipline, but this does not mean generative design is 
solely dependent on computers or GPUs. With generative AI, architecture faces a crossroads 
between rule -based geometric scripting and image- based diffusion workflows. The Vitruvian 
turn signals a return to explicit operations and generative rules that may involve scripts, 
constraint graphs, parametric dependencies, shape grammars, boundary representations, or other 
structured descriptions. What these representations share is not a specific software platform but a 
common property: they make the relations of form available for manipulation.

compelling volumetric fragments, but the resulting objects remain semantically thin and

Image-first diffusion fails this test in four ways. First, it privileges effects over formal and 
mathematical relations, placing phenomenology over computation. The output records what a 
form looks like under certain viewing conditions, not what dependencies produced it. Second, it 
weakens revision. Changes to an image are typically achieved by re -prompting, inpainting, 
reseeding, or adding new conditions rather than by editing a formal structure from within. This 
returns the generative process back to the initial noise that created it. Third, it breaks 
coordination. Even when an image is turned into a mesh, the result rarely carries the semantic 
structure required for robust exchange with analytical, fabrication, or documentation workflows; 
the meshes appear as artifacts or objects closed onto themselves (Figure 6). Fourth, it relocates 
authorship from procedure to curation. The designer becomes highly active in selecting and 
steering outputs, but comparatively inactive at the level of defining the generative object, the 
rules, or the algorithm itself. Thus, the generative aspect of architecture becomes a perceptual 
process rather than a constructive one.  
Diffusion does not fail because it cannot surprise us; it fails because its surprises are difficult to 
translate into revisable architectural form. Images remain useful as references, evaluations, and 
communication devices, and one could argue that diffusion augments the precedent condition by 
making it generative. However, if images remain the ontological ground of design, they 
undermine computation. The more precise alternative is not a total rejection of images, but a 
shift away from representational priority for generative design. Emerging agentic scripting 
frameworks can offer a viable alternative because they attempt to generate or revise code and 
operations rather than merely synthesize visual representations (Rietschel, Guo, and Steinfeld
