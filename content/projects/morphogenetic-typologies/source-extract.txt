Morphogenetic T ypologies
T opical Studio
Project Summary 
Morphogenetic T ypologies investigated historical 
architectural precedents through computational 
and generative methods. The studio focused on 
pre-20th-century, wing-based building types whose 
spatial organization developed through symmetry, 
proportional systems, subdivision, accretion, axial hi-
erarchy, and branching. Rather than treating historical 
buildings as dormant artifacts or stylistic references, 
students studied them as rule-based systems capable of 
producing new architectural variations. 
 
The studio asked students to examine how large ar-
chitectural types organize complexity. Palaces, manor 
houses, pavilion hospitals, asylums, radial prisons, and 
Palladian villas were treated as formal machines: build-
ings whose plans, wings, courtyards, axes, thresholds, 
and circulation structures were shaped by repeatable 
organizational principles. Through this lens, historical 
architecture became a source of computational intelli-
gence. Students were not asked to reproduce historical 
styles, but to extract the abstract rules that allowed 
each typology to grow, stabilize, mutate, and produce 
coherent spatial order. 
 
Methodology 
The semester was organized in two primary stages: 
computational analysis and generative synthesis. 
 
In the first half of the semester, students conducted 
computational analysis of selected precedents. W ork-
ing within typological research groups, they began by 
documenting and reconstructing historical buildings 
through drawings, diagrams, and digital models. This 
phase emphasized abstraction. Students reduced com-
plex architectural plans into legible geometric systems, 
studying how symmetry, ratio, axial organization, 
triangulation, subdivision, repetition, and branching 
shaped each precedent. 
 
This analytical phase asked students to move care-
fully from image to diagram, from diagram to rule, 
and from rule to model. They developed symmetry 
breakdowns, proportional studies, growth diagrams, 
and taxonomies of formal operations. T riangulation 
was used as both an analytical and generative method: 
a way to clarify relationships between centers, wings, 
axes, courtyards, and peripheral zones. Through this 
work, students learned to describe historical architec-
ture not only through form, style, or program, but 
through the deeper formal and organizational proce-
dures that structure typological identity. 
 
The second half of the semester shifted from analysis 
to synthesis. Students translated their precedent stud-
ies into parametric algorithms and generative work-
flows. Using agentic scripting tools such as Cursor and 
Codex, students translated formal rules into recursive 
algorithms and generative design interfaces capable 
of producing controlled variations of their selected 
typologies. The goal was to implement historical rules 
as operative design procedures, allowing each typology 
to be tested through parameter changes, formal muta-
tions, and scripted variation. 
 
This phase treated the algorithm as an architectur-
al research instrument. Students used scripting to 
automate the transformation of typological rules, 
producing families of massing, plan, and spatial 
configurations. Agentic scripting tools supported the 
development of code, debugging, parametric logic, 
and iterative exploration, allowing students to move 
more fluidly between architectural intention and com-
putational implementation. Rather than producing a 
single final design, each project developed a generative 
machine capable of exploring variation for the final 
review.  
 
Students also investigated how AI tools could extend 
architectural morphology through dataset prepara-
tion, synthetic outputs, and the study of variation. 
The emphasis remained architectural: AI and scripting 
were used to test how far typological identity could be 
stretched before it became unstable, unbalanced, or 
transformed into a new formal species. 
 
Research Tracks 
Student projects were organized around historical-
ly significant architectural typologies and extracted 
formal growth principles. Palace studies examined 
grouping, symmetrical hierarchy and recursive seg-
mentation in plan. Manor house projects focused on 
accretion, domestic expansion, asymmetry, chimney 
clustering and wing-based growth. Pavilion hospital 
studies investigated modular repetition, environmen-
tal spacing, and distributed massing strategies. Each 
typology was first analyzed through diagrams that later 
became explicit rules for the generative machines.  
Student Outcomes 
The final work demonstrated a progression from his-
torical analysis to computational authorship. Students 
produced precedent diagrams, abstraction studies, 
symmetry analyses, triangulated mappings, typological 
taxonomies, digital reconstructions, scripted systems, 
parametric models, synthetic outputs, and machine 
matrices. Using agentic scripting and AI-assisted work-
flows, they translated historical architectural knowl-
edge into generative design systems without reducing 
precedent to stylistic imitation. 
 
T ogether, the student work positioned typology as an 
active design engine. Each project began with the close 
study of a historical precedent and transformed its 
underlying organizational logic into a computational 
model capable of generating, varying, and critically 
testing architectural form. The studio connected 
architectural history with contemporary computation-
al practice and emerging artificial intelligence tools, 
showing students how historical morphology can 
support new methods of design research, parametric 
variation, and speculative architectural production.
