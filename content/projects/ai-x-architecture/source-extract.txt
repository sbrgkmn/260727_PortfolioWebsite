GOKMEN TEACHING
AI x Architecture
Elective Seminar
Course Summary 
This elective seminar introduced undergraduate and 
graduate students to emerging artificial intelligence 
workflows for architectural design, visualization, 
scripting, and generative research. The seminar was 
structured as an accessible but technically ambitious 
course, welcoming students with different levels of 
coding, computational design, and AI experience. 
Rather than presenting AI as a singular tool or short-
cut, the course framed it as a shifting ecology of 
methods that can expand architectural authorship, 
representation, and workflow design. 
 
The central goal of the course was to build AI literacy 
through direct experimentation and literature review. 
Students learned to prompt large-language models 
for design ideas, code, and project narratives; produce 
architectural images through text-to-image and im-
age-to-image generation using ComfyUI workflows; 
generate and post-process geometry from prompts 
and images; use AI agents for parametric scripting; 
curate image, text, and geometry datasets; and critical-
ly reflect on the creative, technical, and ethical implica-
tions of AI in architectural practice. 
 
The seminar positioned AI as both a creative partner 
and an object of inquiry. Students were asked not only 
to produce outputs, but to understand how those 
outputs were generated, controlled, curated, and 
interpreted. The course therefore combined technical 
training with critical reflection, asking students to 
develop workflows that could be explained, evaluated, 
and adapted for architectural research and design. 
 
Methodology 
The course was organized as an open workshop en-
vironment supported by lectures, tutorials, template 
files, online repositories, peer learning, and in-class 
technical support. Approximately thirty percent of 
class time was dedicated to lectures, tool demon-
strations, and discussion, while seventy percent was 
structured as workshop-based learning. This format 
allowed students to work at different speeds, revisit 
technical materials through online tutorials, and re-
ceive targeted feedback during class sessions. 
 
Because AI tools evolve quickly, the course empha-
sized adaptability over fixed software mastery. Stu-
dents worked with open-source and cloud-based 
platforms, including ComfyUI, Runpod, remote 
GPU workflows, LLM interfaces, scripting agents, and 
integrated generative tools inside Rhinoceros/Grass-
hopper. Tutorials were designed to give students a 
shared technical foundation, while assignments asked 
them to modify, extend, and critically test workflows 
for architectural purposes. 
 
The seminar also included readings, tool presenta-
tions, and discussions on the history, culture, and 
ethics of artificial intelligence. This helped balance 
technical experimentation with broader disciplinary 
questions: What does AI change about representa-
tion? How does prompting alter authorship? What 
kinds of biases or constraints are embedded in data-
sets? How can architects evaluate machine-generated 
outputs without surrendering design judgment? 
 
Course Structure 
The semester was divided into three primary modules: 
Rendering, Scripting, and Datasets. Each module in-
troduced a different area of AI application and culmi-
nated in a project or assignment sequence. 
 
Module I: AI & Rendering 
The first module focused on image synthesis and 
architectural visualization. Students developed custom 
ComfyUI workflows using text-to-image, image-to-im-
age, diffusion models, ControlNet preprocessors, and 
parametric inputs. W orking individually on selected 
architectural typologies, students produced visualiza-
tion studies through controlled variations. The project 
emphasized technical understanding of inputs and 
outputs, helping students move beyond prompt-based 
image generation toward more deliberate workflow 
construction. 
 
Module II: AI & Scripting 
The second module introduced AI-assisted coding 
and parametric form generation. Students worked 
with scripting agents and large-language models, 
including tools such as Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code, 
ChatGPT, and Gemini, to develop architectural form 
generators in Python, W ebGL, Rhinoceros, or Grass-
hopper. The emphasis was on documenting the evolu-
tion of code, testing parametric variation, and under-
standing how AI agents can support computational 
modeling without replacing architectural intention. 
 
Module III: AI & Datasets 
The final module focused on dataset preparation 
and AI-augmented generative workflows. Students 
framed research questions, curated images, prompts, 
or 3D geometry, and developed datasets that could 
support AI training or generative design experimenta-
tion. This module introduced students to the logic of 
dataset construction, including balance, consistency, 
formatting, and translation between media. Students 
explored workflows involving 3D meshes, LoRA train-
ing, and synthetic architectural content generation. 
 
Student W ork Presented 
The student work from this seminar documents a 
progression from AI-assisted visualization to AI-as-
sisted scripting and dataset-based generative research. 
Projects include custom rendering workflows, image 
variation matrices, scripted architectural generators, 
parametric typology studies, curated datasets, and ex-
perimental workflows that combine images, prompts, 
geometry, and code. 
 
Learning Outcomes 
Students developed practical skills in AI tools while 
strengthening computational and critical thinking that 
helped demystify AI tools. They learned to construct 
and evaluate AI workflows, use LLMs for scripting 
and design support, generate architectural visualiza-
tions through controlled image pipelines, curate data-
sets for machine-learning applications, and reflect on 
the ethical and creative implications of AI in architec-
ture. The course emphasized that AI is most valuable 
when paired with architectural judgment: the ability 
to select, edit, test, interpret, and situate machine-gen-
erated outputs within a larger design process.
