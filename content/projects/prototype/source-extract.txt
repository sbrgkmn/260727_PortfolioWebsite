Pedagogical Premise 
Prototype introduced second-year students to archi-
tecture as a generative discipline. Rather than treating 
design as the production of a singular final object, 
the studio asked students to understand architec-
ture as a process of rule-making, iteration, variation, 
testing, and material translation. Students learned to 
design systems before designing buildings: workflows, 
protocols, assemblies, and geometric rules that could 
generate architectural outcomes. 
 
The studio built on foundational first-year skills while 
expanding students’ technical and conceptual range 
through computational thinking, digital modeling, 
physical construction, and AI-assisted design and 
visualization. Emerging technologies were introduced 
not as shortcuts, but as instruments for speculation, 
representation, and critical authorship. Students used 
computational tools to ask architectural questions, 
test alternatives, and construct design logic through 
drawings, models, scripts, prompts, and fabricated 
assemblies. 
 
The studio also supported the Archetype Sympo-
sium, held at UNC Charlotte on October 24, 2025, 
where student work was exhibited as part of a broader 
conversation on architecture, artificial intelligence, 
computation, authorship, and the future of design. 
 
Methodology 
The semester was structured through two comple-
mentary projects: Bytescape and Foldscape. T ogether, 
these projects introduced students to two opposing 
but related design logics: tectonic and stereotomic. 
The first project moved from parts to whole, asking 
students to generate architecture through aggregation, 
modularity, material assembly, and rule-based growth. 
The second moved from whole to parts, asking stu-
dents to begin with continuous surfaces and translate 
them into folded tectonic systems, programs, and 
inhabitable architectural proposals. 
 
This pairing allowed students to understand archi-
tecture through contrasting modes of production: 
tectonic assembly and stereotomic continuity. In both 
projects, students moved between analog and digital 
methods, working through physical models, paramet-
ric systems, LLM-generated scripting applications,  
drawings, diagrams, and fabrication workflows. The 
emphasis was not only on what students produced, 
but on how they constructed the rules and procedures 
that generated their work. 
 
Project 1: Bytescape 
Bytescape was a tectonic generator for modular archi-
tecture. W orking in groups of four, students explored 
aggregation and emergence through LLM scripting of 
cellular automata systems, parametric variation, ma-
terial constraints, and large-scale collaborative fabrica-
tion. Students generated matrices of tower variations, 
selected and developed one iteration as a continuous 
exhibition space, and translated the design into a 
physical tower model using modular materials (CMU 
blocks and wood strips). 
 
This project emphasized part-to-whole thinking. 
Students learned how simple rules can produce 
complex spatial organizations, how discrete elements 
can become architectural systems, and how material 
constraints shape design intelligence. The final towers 
combined computation, representation, structure, 
circulation, and fabrication into large collaborative 
prototypes. 
 
Project 2: Foldscape 
Foldscape was a stereotomic generator for surface 
architecture. Students began with exploring folding 
techniques, including Miura-ori, V-fold, herringbone, 
Ron Resch, Y oshimura, Kresling, hypar, spiral, and 
waterbomb systems. W orking first in pairs and then 
individually, they developed folding matrices, trans-
lated analog models into architectural surfaces, and 
designed artist pavilions that integrated studio, gallery, 
and residential programs. 
 
This project emphasized whole-to-parts thinking. 
Students explored how continuous surfaces could be 
folded, cut, articulated, and transformed into struc-
ture, enclosure, circulation, and spatial atmosphere. 
AI tools supported the development of artist narra-
tives and conceptual renderings, while digital fabrica-
tion enabled students to translate folded geometries 
into precise physical models. 
 
Student W ork Presented 
The following portfolio pages showcase selected stu-
dent work from Bytescape and Foldscape. The proj-
ects demonstrate how second-year students developed 
architectural proposals through rule-based experimen-
tation, collaborative making, digital modeling, AI-as-
sisted  scripting applications, and physical fabrication. 
T ogether, the two projects show a pedagogical progres-
sion from modular aggregation to folded continuity, 
from abstract systems to inhabitable architectural 
prototypes. 
 
Learning Outcomes 
Students learned to construct design methods through 
rules, variations, and iterative testing; to translate 
computational and analog experiments into archi-
tectural form; and to communicate design intent 
through drawings, models, diagrams, and visual 
narratives. They developed technical proficiency in 
digital modeling, parametric workflows, AI-assisted 
design generation (LLMs), and digital fabrication, 
while also strengthening collaboration, critique, time 
management, and architectural decision-making. The 
studio positioned second-year design education as an 
experimental laboratory where foundational architec-
tural skills are expanded through emerging tools and 
generative thinking.

Bytescape Phase I: Cellular Automata + Scripting  
with LLMs 
Students developed generative scripts inside Rhinoc-
eros using Python to explore Cellular Automata as a 
rule-based design method. W orking with large lan-
guage models such as ChatGPT, Gemini, and Copi-
lot, students used AI-assisted scripting to generate, 
test, debug, and refine parametric algorithms. The goal 
was not simply to produce forms, but to understand 
how simple local rules could generate complex spa-
tial organizations. Each group produced a matrix of 
formal variations, documented the rules behind their 
outputs, and selected key samples for 3D printing as 
physical studies of computational aggregation.
Mackenzie Dolle + Hunter Shaffer + Michael W allace + Hadley Melton

Bytescape Phase II: Design Exploration +  
Continuous Circulation 
In the second phase, students transformed their ab-
stract Cellular Automata volumes into programmatic 
spatial studies. Each group selected one generated 
massing and developed it as a continuous exhibition 
space, integrating distinct program volumes through 
ramps, stairs, corridors, and sectional connections. 
The emphasis shifted from formal generation to 
architectural inhabitation: students were asked to 
test how computational massing could accommodate 
spatial hierarchy, circulation, structure, and experi-
ential sequence. This phase also introduced material 
translation, as students developed scaled basswood 
models to study how their digital geometries could be 
reinterpreted through wood construction, detailing, 
and assembly logic preparing them for the final phase.
Olivia Dreyfus + Mo Pirela + Kaylie V ong + Iterland W ells

Phase III: Digital Fabrication Strategies + Tower 
Construction 
In the final phase, each group selected one basswood 
design study to develop into a full tower prototype. 
Students shifted from spatial exploration to fabrica-
tion strategy, using part analysis, annotated drawings, 
material detailing, and assembly logic to translate their 
generated massing into a constructible model. W ork-
ing within a fixed 24” x 24” x 72” volume, groups 
developed tower structures using CMUs and wood 
strips as the primary construction system. This phase 
emphasized the relationship between computational 
form, material constraint, structural stability, and fab-
rication planning, requiring students to resolve how 
abstract generative geometries could become physical 
assemblies through sequencing, detailing, and collabo-
rative construction.
Fabrizio Farfan + Sabrina Coffaro + Paige Reed + Rishika Turlapaty
Jameson Jensen + David Kushnir + Christian Richter + Agnes Rodts
