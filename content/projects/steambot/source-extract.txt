Project Description
SteamBot investigates how computational design and 
industrial robotics can extend steam bending, one 
of the oldest timber craft techniques. Conventional 
steam bending softens wood through heat and mois-
ture, shapes it around a mold, clamps it into position, 
and allows it to cure. The process carries deep material 
intelligence, but it is constrained by fixed formwork. 
Each new curvature often requires a new jig, mold, 
or clamping setup, making variation slow, labor-in-
tensive, and difficult to scale. SteamBot begins from 
this limitation and asks how robotic fabrication can 
produce freeform timber components while preserv-
ing wood’s grain, flexibility, springback, and organic 
behavior.
The project proposes a jigless robotic steam-bending 
workflow for producing modular timber members 
from linear wood strips. Rather than treating timber 
as passive stock to be cut or milled after geometry has 
already been defined, SteamBot works during the brief 
interval when wood becomes flexible. In this softened 
state, steam, grain direction, humidity, force, timing, 
springback, and elastic memory all shape the final 
geometry. The central research question is how to cre-
ate a computational process that negotiates between 
robotic precision and material response rather than 
simply forcing a predetermined form onto wood.
Research Context
The project is positioned within computational 
timber design, robotic fabrication, and material 
computation. Much existing work in robotic timber 
fabrication focuses on milling, cutting, drilling, or 
assembling timber after it has stabilized. SteamBot 
shifts the emphasis toward robotic forming while 
the material is actively changing state. This requires 
a different relationship between digital control and 
physical behavior. Instead of assuming that geometry 
can be imposed onto matter, the project treats wood 
as responsive, resistant, and partially authorial.
SteamBot also responds to growing architectural 
interest in mass timber, low-waste fabrication, and 
sustainable material systems. By producing custom 
curved members without disposable molds or fixed 
formwork, the project explores a flexible and material-
ly efficient approach to non-standard timber fabrica-
tion. It links architectural research, ecological material 
practice, craft knowledge, and robotic manufacturing.
Objectives
The funded proposal was organized around four ob-
jectives: to explore computational methods for robotic 
steam bending; to study material behavior through 
steam, humidity, grain orientation, thickness, cur-
vature, and mechanical response; to develop robotic 
bending techniques using KUKA robotic arms and 
Parametric Robot Control; and to disseminate the 
resulting knowledge through prototypes, parametric 
workflows, an interdisciplinary fabrication course, and 
publication or exhibition outcomes.

verge around a larger ambition: transforming timber

Methods
The project is structured through three work pack-
ages: material characterization, robot integration and 
programming, and parametric design. Material charac-
terization studies timber species, strip thickness, grain 
direction, curvature, elasticity, humidity, bendability, 
springback, and breakage limits. These observations 
inform a computational model that can guide bending 
behavior and assess the feasibility of different curva-
tures.
Robot integration focuses on translating desired 
curves into robotic movement sequences using KUKA 
robotic arms and KUKA|prc. The setup tests path ac-
curacy, rotation, timing, reach limits, and the effects of 
humidity on the bending process. Parametric design 
connects fabrication research to architectural out-
comes by developing modular components, joinery 
systems, assemblies, and larger spatial configurations.
Material and Digital W orkflows
SteamBot operates through two overlapping loops: a 
digital workflow and a material workflow. The digital 
workflow begins in Rhinoceros and Grasshopper, 
where target member curves are extracted and con-
verted into KUKA|prc robotic motion. The material 
workflow begins with cutting linear wood strips, 
steaming them according to species and thickness, 
bending them through robotic movement, transfer-
ring them into radial drying forms, and assembling 
them into larger configurations.
The critical exchange occurs during fabrication, where 
an abstract spline becomes an angle-controlled robotic 
operation and a softened wood strip becomes an archi-
tectural member. This exchange defines the project’s 
central contribution: a workflow in which compu-
tational design and material behavior are developed 
together rather than sequentially.

This material phase demonstrates that robotic timber 
bending cannot be reduced to toolpath generation 
alone. The success of the process depends on species 
behavior, moisture content, steaming duration, strip 
thickness, robotic timing, temporary fixation, and the 
choreography between material readiness and robotic 
motion.
Robotic Fabrication W orkflow
The robotic workflow tested three movement families: 
planar bending through XZ rotation, axial twisting 
through XY rotation along the wood profile, and 
compound double-curvature bending through coor-
dinated XY and XZ rotations. These tests allowed the 
project to move beyond simple arcs toward members 
with more complex curvature and spatial variation.
Custom Grasshopper scripts translated target geome-
try into angle-based robot paths, shifting the workflow 
from fixed forming infrastructure to variable robotic 
motion, drying angles, and modular attachment 
points. The resulting members can be reconfigured 
into columns, walls, pavilion structures, or larger mod-
ular assemblies, demonstrating how robotic steam 
bending can support both material experimentation 
and architectural design research.
