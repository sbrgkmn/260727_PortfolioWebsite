Pedagogical Premise 
Gothic Suprematist Machine was structured as a de-
sign research studio that treated architectural history 
as an active computational material. Rather than 
asking students to reproduce Gothic architecture as 
style, the studio asked them to inspect Gothic build-
ings as complex formal bodies: systems of symmetry, 
proportion, hierarchy, repetition, subdivision, and 
articulation. Students studied Gothic architecture 
through plans, vaults, towers, spires, sectional bands, 
and surface systems, then transformed these observa-
tions into abstract rules for contemporary architectur-
al production. 
 
The studio paired this historical inquiry with Su-
prematism, particularly its legacy of abstraction, com-
position, and spatial projection. Suprematism provid-
ed a language for moving away from literal precedent 
and toward geometric extraction. Gothic architecture 
provided the disciplinary depth: a dense archive of 
structural rhythm, spatial sequence, verticality, orna-
ment, and part-to-whole organization. Between these 
two poles, students learned to translate historical mor-
phology into diagrams, taxonomies, digital models, 
physical artifacts, and generative design systems. 
 
The central pedagogical goal was to help students un-
derstand that computational design is not only a tech-
nical skill, but also a way of constructing architectural 
knowledge. Modeling, scripting, and variation were 
introduced as methods for reading precedent, testing 
formal rules, and generating speculative futures from 
historical material. 
 
Methodology 
The studio unfolded through a sequence of research, 
abstraction, and generative production. Students 
began with collective precedent analysis, developing 
drawings and diagrams that identified how Gothic ele-
ments are organized, repeated, and transformed across 
buildings. This work established a shared archive of 
morphological research. 
 
In the second phase, students moved from analysis to 
abstraction. They translated Gothic systems into Su-
prematist compositions, triangulated surfaces, mass-
ing studies, and tectonic rules. Modeling workshops 
introduced workflows for mesh modeling, geometric 
articulation, and formal variation. Students were 
asked to move carefully between representation and 
rule-making: each model was not only an object, but 
also evidence of an underlying design logic. 
 
In the final phase, students developed individual gen-
erative machines. These projects used computational 
scripts to produce families of formal variations derived 
from their earlier research. The emphasis shifted from 
single-authored objects to systems capable of produc-
ing multiple outcomes. Through this process, stu-
dents connected precedent analysis, visual abstraction, 
and computational authorship. 
 
Phase I: Gothic Suprematism 
W eeks 1–4 focused on precedent research, symmetry 
breakdown, parts-to-whole analysis, and taxonomy. 
Students worked in groups to study Gothic architec-
tural systems and develop a shared body of drawings, 
diagrams, and models. 
 
Phase II: Suprematist Form Wrapping 
W eeks 5–10 focused on abstraction, mesh modeling, 
detailing, geometric variation, and model matrices. 
Students developed individual formal studies that 
translated Gothic elements into abstracted surfaces, 
masses, and compositional systems. 
 
Phase III: Generative Suprematism 
W eeks 11–16 focused on scripting, Gothic mor-
phogenesis, generative machines, and final variation 
matrices. Students developed computational systems 
capable of producing speculative architectural varia-
tions from their rule sets. 
 
Student Project Tracks 
The student work presented in the following pages is 
organized around four primary research tracks. 
Triforium: Projects in this track investigated Gothic 
interiors as a system of repetition, branching, group-
ing and abstract ornament. Students treated the trifo-
rium as a zone where surface, structure, and percep-
tion meet, translating its rhythmic logic into abstract 
façades and networked rib structures. 
 
Spires / Towers: This track focused on verticality, 
aggregation, tapering, silhouette, and sectional hi-
erarchy. Students used towers and spires as models 
for exploring height, stacking and structural rhythm 
through recursion and variation.
Cathedral Plans: These projects studied axial 
organization, bay rhythm, nave and transept relation-
ships, thresholds, and spatial sequencing. Students 
transformed plan-based analysis into rule systems for 
generating new spatial and formal compositions. 
V aults: Projects in this track examined ribbed vaults, 
webbing, structural subdivision, and triangulated 
surface logic. Students translated vault geometry into 
abstract spatial fields and generative systems through 
recursive subdivision. 
 
Learning Outcomes 
By the end of the studio, students were able to analyze 
historical precedents through formal and computa-
tional lenses, construct taxonomies of architectural 
parts, translate morphological observations into design 
rules, and generate variations using digital models and 
scripts. The projects demonstrate student learning 
across research, abstraction, computation, represen-
tation, and iterative design. T ogether, they show how 
historical architectural knowledge can be transformed 
into speculative design systems without reducing 
history to imitation.
